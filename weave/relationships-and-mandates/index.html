Run a relationship across several mandates

Consider the team that prepares regular reports for the customer whose export failed. The customer now wants a data integration as well. It sounds promising, until access to the data falls through. What should the team do next? It still owes the reports. It needs to resolve the access problem before it can take on the integration. And someone needs to explain both to the customer.

Weave keeps that whole relationship in view: what has been promised, what is still being considered and who is carrying each part forward. The service is not built yet; this example follows how its commercial records help a team work through those decisions.

What have we already promised?

The reports belong to an agreed service commitment, which Weave calls a mandate. The integration is an opportunity: work the team is considering, with no delivery promise yet. Both belong to the customer's account. Each report also needs its own work item, so the person preparing it and the person reviewing it can follow that report through to acceptance.

The account manager looks after the continuing relationship. An account executive carries a particular opportunity or mandate. In a small team, the same person might do both; a larger account might have several executives and delivery teams. Weave records each person's participation and when it applies. A person working on several accounts keeps one record, with their roles connected to each account. When a new contact takes over, the history should still show who made an earlier assessment or promise.

Those roles help the team find the person to talk to. Permission to commit delivery or accept a result still comes from the authority given to that person for the work. The account executive may be pursuing the integration while someone else decides whether the delivery team can take it on.

The access falls through

At discovery, the sponsor describes delays and the operating owner offers access to the records. That gives the team a problem to investigate and a way to investigate it. When access becomes unavailable, the opportunity is blocked. The reports continue under their existing mandate.

At the next review, the team needs to explain why its assessment changed. Weave keeps the original assessment and its supporting evidence alongside the later one: what was known, what pointed the other way, when the information was gathered and how much confidence it supported. The criteria used, the assessor and the reviewer's decision belong with each assessment. Calls, emails, meetings and research can supply that evidence, with their sources and access rules preserved.

Suppose the customer later offers a smaller dataset. That may make a narrower integration worth considering. The team can assess it afresh, and the new assessment can supersede the old one. The earlier reasoning remains available to anyone asking why the work did not proceed sooner.

Important

Check whether the team may take the work at all before you score it. A high score never overrides a forbidden use or any other disqualifying condition.

The decision should tell the team what to do next. A blocked opportunity has an obstacle to resolve, such as the missing access. A deferred one waits for a stated condition or review date. A rejected one keeps the reason for declining. A superseded assessment points to the newer judgment while preserving the grounds for the earlier one. These distinctions matter when an interested customer returns: the team can see whether to investigate, wait or explain why it declined.

For an account review, a read-only export brings together the outstanding reviews, missing or old evidence and changes since the last review. Updates are still made in the records themselves. The relationship inside the account develops why that history is more useful than a contact list and a score.

Agreeing the next piece of work

The smaller dataset may be enough for a workshop. The sponsor suggests one; someone authorised to commit the delivery team then needs to agree what it will produce, who will do it and who can accept the result. Once agreed, the workshop gets its own work item and evidence. From a request to accepted work follows the assignment, review and handover.

If another provider will run the workshop, the client offers the work and the provider decides whether to accept it. Each keeps its own records. The provider reviews what it delivers, and the client separately decides whether that result meets its request. Recording a contact in the account gives that person no internal access or approval role; the developer guide to instance exchange explains how the two organisations exchange work.

The account manager can now return to the customer with a useful answer: the reports are continuing, the smaller dataset gives the integration another chance, and the workshop needs an agreed commitment before anyone owes its delivery. In the relationship itself, the useful question stays the same: who owes the next result, under which commitment, and who may accept it?
