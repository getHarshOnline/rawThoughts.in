Radio became Spotify

On this page

My grandfather listened to songs on a transistor. My father listens on a phone. The music still plays. The person listening still wants something familiar, something surprising, something that keeps him company. But between those two devices, an entire arrangement of people, businesses and habits has changed.

TV didn't go away. It just became YouTube. Radio became Spotify. Cable became on-demand services such as Netflix. The newspaper became LinkedIn; the magazine became Instagram. The need survived; the way of meeting it opened possibilities that the old arrangement could barely accommodate. That is the lens I use when thinking about AI and a business going through its own transition.

It is easy to tell this story as a parade of faster chips and smaller screens. I grew up with Doordarshan and a roof antenna that needed adjusting. Now each family member can choose a programme, carry it in a pocket, reply to its creator and become a creator themselves. The interesting change is what became possible between the person watching and the person making it. A horizontal flip became a vertical scroll. We changed how we reached the world, and that changed how the world reached us.

When the measure changes

A broadcast programme brings an audience to a time slot. An on-demand service lets someone choose a programme and a time. A creator can publish for a particular audience and hear back from it. Each arrangement gives the people running it different decisions to make, and different things they can observe.

Marketing moved from space in print and a cost per word to keyword bids and advertising built around particular audiences. In that change, the business question moved from TRP toward CAC: from the audience a programme reached to the cost of acquiring a customer. How many people watched? Who responded? What did it cost to bring in someone who pays? When distribution changes, a business can follow more of that path and organise itself around the parts it can now see. Engagement, reach and acquisition cost become operating questions, changing both what the business measures and what it tries to improve.

This is how an interface change grows into a business change. A personal screen makes personal choice possible. That choice produces a different kind of feedback. Feedback changes recommendations and advertising; those change what gets made, who can make a living making it, and what the audience expects next. A new attention economy grows around an old human appetite.

The tempting mistake is to keep the old operating arrangement and add the new interface. Put the same scheduled programme on a smaller screen. Put the same form behind a chatbot. You have changed where the customer presses the button. Have you changed what the customer can do?

The front desk needs the business behind it

In Evolution of AI and its future in B2B, I imagined an AI front desk: a customer could say what they needed instead of learning the company's software. The front desk would draw on what the organisation knew, and the organisation would learn from what happened at the front desk. Distribution, retention, control, efficiency and contracts would all have to develop around that relationship.

Consider a customer whose export has failed. “I have passed this to the team” is an easy answer to generate. Getting the customer a usable export requires more. Someone needs to know what failed, who can investigate, what may be changed and which result will satisfy the customer. If the investigation discovers an old product decision, the reason for that decision matters too. A fluent answer cannot supply a reason the organisation has already forgotten.

Now imagine that the customer can describe the problem, the system can find the relevant history, and the people doing the work can agree the next step in that same conversation. The customer need has stayed the same. The arrangement around it has changed: less re-entry, fewer handovers that lose their purpose, and a shorter path between a question and someone able to act on it.

That is the question behind Weave's design for relationships and work. A conversation can carry a commitment while still being a conversation. The system has to preserve who agreed, what they agreed to and what happened afterwards. Moving a card is a small part of that job. Making the commitment intelligible to the next person is the part that lasts.

For a business considering AI, this changes where the work begins. Start with one costly process and the people who understand it. Follow a real request through to its accepted result. Where does someone have to reconstruct context? Where does the same decision get made again? Where can nobody explain an exception? Those are places where better intelligence might matter, provided the process can make use of it.

What happens to what we remember?

There is another side to the transistor and the phone. A better tool also changes what we practise doing for ourselves. I no longer need to remember every phone number or every turn on a route. The convenience is real. So is the dependency when the information is needed and the tool cannot supply it.

An organisation can do the same thing with its memory. The code still runs, but nobody knows why that feature exists. The document is there, but its conclusion has become detached from the question it answered. A summary travels faster than the conversation that qualified it. Then we ask an AI to work from the summaries and wonder why its confident answer misses the point.

Faster answers can make this worse. The easier it becomes to obtain the next output, the less time we may spend understanding the previous one. Faster, cheaper, better becomes an expectation; that expectation encourages the next tool, which makes the pace feel ordinary again. By the time we look up, the habits and institutions around the work may have changed more than the latest product announcement suggests.

I want to use these tools. I am building them because they can let me pursue more of the questions I care about. The difficulty is carrying the learning forward while the machinery changes. What was tried? Why did it fail? What should the next attempt keep? If every new tool makes us reconstruct that account, we are buying speed by repeatedly losing the explanation.

A publication is a small example

The same problem appears in the writing you are reading. One idea may belong in a standalone essay, help explain a product and become a step in a longer reading journey. Copy the paragraph into three places and each copy can gradually acquire a different meaning. Keep only a private note and the reader never gets the explanation at all.

Gazette gives an article one source while allowing several reading paths to lead to it. The writer still has to do the intellectual work: decide who the explanation is for, keep its assumptions visible, and write a different treatment when a different reader needs one. The publishing system can preserve the relationship between those pieces. It cannot decide that two superficially similar arguments mean the same thing.

Pale addresses the broader problem of agents working across a declared world: what they are doing, what they can use and which decisions they may make. What I want from it is context and responsibility that carry across the work, so that each faster action does not leave the next person with less understanding.

Follow the change through

The radio-to-Spotify lens asks three connected questions. What need persists? What does the new arrangement make possible? And what does using it change about us and the business around it?

Those questions reach beyond a single workflow. If an organisation needs fewer people to produce the same result, where do the savings go? What do its customers now have the money and time to buy? Post-AGI Economics follows that question into income, ownership and demand. A technical improvement has an economic life after the demo.

For the person running a business today, the useful starting point is close at hand. Look at a customer request that ought to be simple and follow what actually happens. Then ask what you would build if the customer could state the need directly, the right history could be found and the people responsible could act without reconstructing the whole story. Keep following it until the result reaches the customer and the organisation learns from it.

My grandfather wanted to hear a song. My father still does. The need gives us continuity. Everything we build around it is where the next possibility begins.
