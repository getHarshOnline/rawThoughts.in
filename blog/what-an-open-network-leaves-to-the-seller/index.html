What an open network leaves to the seller

  4. Data access cannot be assumed from openness

An open commerce network can widen the route between a seller and a buyer without making the sale easy or profitable. Unbundling changes who coordinates discovery, payment and fulfilment. It does not remove the need for coordination.

That possibility is especially interesting in agriculture, where discovery and fulfilment are tightly coupled. A wider route to buyers could help a farmer-producer organisation (FPO) find demand for a harvest. Whether it creates income depends on the work between being found and being paid: grading, aggregation, delivery, losses and the terms of the sale.

Access, visibility and conversion are different stages

A catalogue can be technically discoverable without being prominently presented. A product can be presented without being trusted. A trusted product can still be unavailable at the buyer's location or uneconomic to deliver.

That makes marketing a more specific problem than "get onto the network". The seller needs accurate descriptions, useful images, reliable availability and a reason for the buyer to choose the product. The buyer-facing application still shapes how choices are presented. Opening the connection does not establish equal attention for every catalogue.

In an ONDC-style arrangement, the buyer application presents the buying experience, the seller application brings the seller's catalogue and order handling, and discovery connects the request to participating sellers. The FPO still has produce to grade, aggregate and release; a logistics relationship still has an order to carry. The customer does not experience a diagram of participants; they experience whether the promise was clear and whether the order arrived. Coordination failures remain product failures at that boundary.

Agriculture puts fulfilment inside the offer

For produce, a catalogue needs to connect grade, pack size, harvest timing and shelf life to what can actually be delivered. A photograph and an origin story can help a buyer understand the product. They do not establish its condition on arrival. Consider an illustrative farmer-producer organisation that can combine small growers into a dependable order. The work includes stock discipline, grading, packaging and a usable collection schedule. If the catalogue claims availability before those arrangements exist, successful promotion can create cancellations rather than income.

Bulk procurement, household purchases and the sale of farming inputs are also different buying situations. They need different quantities, assurances and fulfilment. Treating "agriculture" as one market loses the distinction that would make a specialist service useful.

For a hypothetical harvest, suppose an FPO can release forty crates on Monday, twenty suitable for a buyer's specified grade and twenty for another market. A campaign promising forty crates of the first grade creates an apparent stock shortage even though the physical stock count was correct. If the buyer needs Thursday delivery and the packing and transport plan consumes the available shelf-life margin, extending promotion to farther destinations can turn more demand into more rejection. The useful catalogue connects a grade to its quantity, release date, pack and feasible delivery window, rather than attaching one availability badge to the crop.

Seasonality changes the marketing job as well. A harvest peak and a festival buying occasion can briefly overlap, but the next week may have another supply mix and another buyer. A static annual demand estimate will not tell the FPO when to aggregate, whom to approach or which orders it should decline. Coordinating those intervals is part of the service a specialist might supply; its value has to appear in fulfilled orders and what the seller keeps.

The rural delivery barrier makes this concrete. A small order may be cheap to produce and expensive to ship, especially where the available service’s minimum charged weight is much larger than the parcel. Aggregating orders can reduce that mismatch, but changes collection times and the freshness promise. A cheaper connection helps only if it supports the pack, route and delivery interval the seller can actually offer.

The available connections have also changed. The Department of Posts reported its first ONDC logistics order booked on 13 January 2026 and delivered on 15 January. Its account describes a “Click & Book” arrangement for pickup, booking, tracking and delivery. That gives the logistics question a concrete new route to examine. One delivery does not establish the cost or spoilage performance for a particular FPO’s recurring produce orders; the channel comparison still needs those observations.

What reaches the seller

Suppose a hypothetical order brings in 100 currency units. The seller spends 15 on delivery, 5 on packaging, 10 on participant and promotional charges and an expected 8 on spoilage, returns and support. That leaves 62 before production cost and other obligations. A lower headline commission can coexist with a worse return if the other costs rise.

The eight-unit expected loss is an allowance across comparable orders. An individual order may lose more or less. For perishables, that variation matters: a channel that looks attractive on average may expose the FPO to occasional batches it cannot afford to lose.

This accounting illustration makes net realisation visible. The comparison is between channels carrying the same product under the same service conditions, not between their headline commissions. A larger campaign can make matters worse if its additional orders travel farther, require smaller packs or produce more returns.

A marketing service adds its own fee and support burden. Its business case needs evidence that the seller retains more value after paying it. A large count of potential sellers does not establish that those sellers can pay, will pay or can be served profitably.

Data access cannot be assumed from openness

Network-health information, a participant's analytics and private buyer transactions are different sources. A protocol that connects participants does not automatically make all their data available for targeting, forecasting or resale. The permitted data also determines the service that can realistically be built. Cross-application attribution may be incomplete. A seller may see an order without seeing every stage that preceded it. A proposed reputation or loyalty feature needs actual cooperation and permitted records, not just a belief that portability would be useful.

For agricultural provenance, more disclosure is not always better. Exact farm locations, yields, images and member records can expose people while helping a buyer understand origin. The design should distinguish what is necessary for the claim from what is merely available to collect.

Community is a relationship, not a free distribution channel

A useful farmer community can create trust and recurring participation. Maintaining it requires expertise, moderation and often on-ground help. A business may pay to reach that audience, but the audience exists because people receive value, not because a slide calls it a funnel. Moving that model into an open network changes the relationships. The participants may not share one audience, one data controller or one promotional interface. What works for a platform with its own community is a hypothesis to test elsewhere, not a blueprint that transfers intact.

Krishify gives a concrete comparison. Its public offering brings crop- and geography-focused communities, advisory and farmer meetings together with business services such as lead generation and marketing. The mechanism is a continuing relationship around a farmer’s work, with commercial opportunities built around that relationship. A participant connecting to ONDC does not automatically acquire such an audience or the trust that sustains it. The comparison suggests something to build and test, rather than an audience to count as already won.

Where specialist help could earn its place

There are several different businesses inside this opportunity. Specialist marketing help could improve catalogues, regional-language descriptions and interpretation of the analytics a seller can actually obtain. A buyer application would take on a different job: attracting buyers and giving them a dependable purchasing experience. Technology supplied to participants might support traceability or coordination, with the integration and data agreements that work requires. Their economics differ because they undertake different parts of the sale.

What interests me is where a specialist could make a completed order more valuable to both sides. Better catalogue information may reduce a buyer's uncertainty; a suitable pack and collection schedule may make an order deliverable; an honest channel comparison may reveal why apparently cheaper access is costing the seller more. An open network creates room for that work. The business exists only where someone benefits enough from its coordination to pay for it.
