The Blue Pill: if you can’t win AI, survive

On this page
  1. What happens to the service business?
  2. Win the distribution that sells outcomes
  3. More experiments, faster contact with reality
  4. The bitter pill: you cannot win all distribution

… and you'll eventually win if you are winning.

Every industry has some form of a power law. In December 2023, I expected AI to skew the rewards disproportionately, making it harder to get a foothold even as it made production easier. The predictions that follow belong to that moment. So how does a business owner keep going within the game as its rules become less comfortable?

Take content creation.

A channel with a million subscribers gets the brand deals. A channel with nine gets none. Yes, that's a deliberately sharp contrast; a small specialist audience can be valuable too. But nobody starts with a million subscribers, and an established distribution gives the next piece of work an advantage that the nine-subscriber channel has to fight to obtain.

For the sake of the calculus, imagine each subscriber has an attention pool. Every view they give your channel spends an attention token from it. There is no cryptocurrency involved here. It's a way of thinking about finite attention and the competing claims on it.

As more content is created, there are more opportunities to ask for those tokens. But the share of any one person's attention that you can capture gets squeezed. Producing more is easier; persuading the same person to spend more of their day with you is a different matter.

Now add AI to that unequal starting position. More published work gives a creator more observations about what people respond to. More distribution gives each new experiment a quicker response. Those observations can inform the next piece of work, and AI can help produce and vary it. Production, feedback and distribution reinforce one another.

The creator with nine subscribers can use the same kind of tool. They do not thereby inherit the million-subscriber channel's history, access or speed of feedback. The larger channel can also offer a brand partner a stronger route to an outcome. Getting a deal can make the next deal easier to get. Getting none leaves you without that particular advantage.

AI did not invent this concentration of rewards. My concern was that it would accelerate it.

What happens to the service business?

Now run that argument through an agency or a software business.

The marginal cost of creating another design, piece of content, collateral or portion of software was, in my expectation, going to move towards something close to zero. Not literally zero. There would still be work, infrastructure and consequences. But enough of the production could become cheap that the commercial expectation would change.

For a particular task, when would AI become inexpensive enough that the old way was no longer a feasible competing offer? I saw the direction as a “when”, not an “if”. The ordering of tasks along that path mattered more than pretending that all of them would change on the same day.

That would push the number of experiments expected per launch up, and the time allowed to reach market down. A customer who can obtain ten plausible variants cheaply will ask why you only tried one. If production is no longer the scarce part, billing for the effort of producing an artefact becomes harder to defend.

The deal starts moving towards the outcome.

What happened after the launch? Did the work produce the result the customer came for? As instrumentation improves, it becomes harder to bullshit your way past that question. The operations involved still matter, but the customer's willingness to pay need not track how many hours you spent performing them.

I expected that pressure to pull down familiar SaaS subscription prices and agency retainers, whether because the costs actually fell or because customers came to believe that they had. Those are different causes with a similar negotiating consequence. The customer expects more for the old price.

More of the contract would therefore concern the result. You would be paid for the trust someone had in your ability to reach it, and for being on call to pacify them when it was “not there yet”. That last bit is less glamorous than a generated demo. It is also much of the relationship.

B2B would begin to absorb more of the tendencies familiar from B2C: the struggle for attention, the expectation of a quicker response and the power of a recognisable voice. So how the hell does one survive in that market?

Win the distribution that sells outcomes

Like it or not, I thought the most consequential move was to win distribution that could sell the outcome.

There was a time when you had websites, and now, you have content distribution.

No one is out there discovering you via your website. SEO nerds will have you believing anything. It can sound like a desperate cry for help.

Waiting for a website to do the work of a relationship is the problem. “We have a site” does not explain who will encounter the business, why they will care or what will earn their trust. Nor does a visit establish that someone paid attention. The argument is not improved by treating discovery, arrival and persuasion as the same event.

Ads give you a more visible mechanism to buy that arrival. They can also make an agency's growth model easier to sell: spend this, measure that, iterate. My argument in 2023 was that ads were becoming more expensive and harder to crack. A black box controlled by a platform remains a black box controlled by a platform. You pay to take part in somebody else's system, under conditions you do not fully control.

Consider an agency proudly presenting a 7% conversion rate from that black box. Are you kidding me? I don't blame them. It's friggin hard. The figure was a rhetorical example, not an industry benchmark or a universal target. Its denominator and the value of the conversion would matter enormously in a real engagement.

Organic growth is harder again, and harder to price as a service. You cannot just promise to buy a predictable amount of someone else's distribution. You have to make work that people choose to encounter, recognise and return to.

No matter how you arrange the commercials, sales is about monetising trust. Branding is about building it. If attention is already divided among a thousand competing demands, a website sitting at the end of a link has very little of it to work with. You have to distribute more, learn what people value and keep earning those tokens until every encounter no longer begins from nothing.

The business has to confront that work. A technically discoverable page and a business people trust answer different parts of the same problem. The point survives whichever search or recommendation surface brings the person to you: what have you done to make the arrival mean something?

More experiments, faster contact with reality

The production-cost argument comes back here. A lower cost per experiment means more experiments per launch. The market's expectation of cheap production puts pressure on your price. An outcome promise puts pressure on the time it takes you to learn what works.

Eventually one of those experiments may help you land “that deal”. Then the deal count grows, and the feedback you can use grows with it.

I expected more of the routine work to become automated and some forms of human error to fall, bringing a capable team to its outcome faster. The expertise still had to be there. If you had it, the tools could help you deliver. If you didn't, your delusion would meet reality faster than you could type “outcome”.

And all of that was going to happen after you had won enough distribution to get the opportunity.

That is why a reusable mechanism can matter to a service business. Work done for one engagement may teach the team something it can apply again, without making a client's records or particular design everybody else's property. DAO Studio's account of turning service work into reusable capability develops that operational question: what can accumulate between engagements, and what must remain with the partner? Reuse can improve the ability to deliver. Distribution still has to earn the next conversation.

The bitter pill: you cannot win all distribution

You cannot be everywhere. You cannot win everyone's attention. The survival argument therefore ends in focused, hyper-specific niches.

The calculus changes when a small group cares deeply about a particular problem. Nine subscribers drawn at random and nine people who share a costly, urgent problem are not the same starting position. The niche does not remove the need for trust. It gives that work a more definite subject and a more plausible audience.

That also leads to an uncomfortable valuation prediction. If a business's realistically reachable market was much smaller than its story suggested, I expected the willingness to accept outrageous price-to-earnings ratios to disappear. A claimed total addressable market would have to meet the resources actually available to win it. These were conditional expectations about how the business landscape might change, not a measured market result or a valuation rule supplied by AI.

With limited resources, survival would become a matter of dominating a useful niche's distribution. Keep improving at the margin until the industry's concentration of rewards begins working in your favour. If you are still surviving as an agency and keep winning deals, those deals can make further deals easier. The same reinforcing pattern can work for a product subscription. It can also work against you when the opportunities keep shrinking.

That is the blue pill. Keep playing, get specific about the outcome, earn the trust and survive long enough for the work to compound.

There is another option: The Red Pill. It asks why the framework for exchanging value has to remain organised around jobs and the same competitive game at all. This essay asks how a business survives inside it. The next asks what else we might choose to build.
