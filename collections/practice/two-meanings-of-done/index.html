Two meanings of done

The comparison arrives on time. The file opens, the tables are readable, and the analyst has handed over what they promised. The recipient opens it to make a decision and finds that it compares the wrong population.

Suppose this was the comparison you commissioned. You have the file, but you still cannot make the decision. The analyst needs to know what arrived and what needs correcting. Calling the whole thing "done" or "not done" gives neither of you enough to continue.

The file arrived. Did it answer the need?

Delivery concerns the promised artifact; acceptance concerns the agreed question and criteria. The recipient may accept the comparison, reject it with reasons or identify a specific piece of work still missing. Those possibilities give the handoff a future: the delivered file remains available, and the particular failure can become a defined revision rather than another ambiguous request to "fix it".

Suppose the decision concerns renewal among existing customers, but the table combines them with people trying the product for the first time. The recipient can say: “The file arrived. I need the existing-customer cohort separated before this can support the renewal decision.” That response recognises the delivery and gives the analyst a specific correction. “This is useless” would discard the useful part; “done” would carry the wrong population into the next decision.

A child task does not close its parent's goal

An application can report that it wrote a file. A deployment system can report that it uploaded a build. Neither alone proves that the intended reader can use the document or that the approved website is being served correctly. The parent goal has its own completion conditions. It can use the child's evidence without replacing those conditions with the easiest observable success signal. Otherwise a green step conceals unfinished work at the next boundary.

Acceptance must belong to someone

In the renewal example, the operating owner responsible for the decision applies the agreed criterion: existing customers, the relevant renewal period and an explanation of who was excluded. The analyst revises the cohort, preserves the original delivered comparison and sends the corrected version with its changed denominator. If the criterion is met, the owner can accept that comparison as sufficient for the renewal analysis. The subsequent decision about what to offer those customers is still another act.

This also limits a disagreement. If the recipient now wants a different period or a trial-customer analysis, that can become new work rather than an endlessly moving acceptance test. A partial acceptance can close the correct cohort table while leaving one disputed interpretation open. Naming the version, criterion and remaining question lets both people act without pretending that the entire business decision fitted inside a file-delivery event.

The supplier has a decision too

The analyst's team may review its own work before delivery. It can confirm that the calculation follows the chosen method, the file contains the requested tables and the stated exclusions are visible. That is a useful acceptance on the supplier's side. It does not give the team the customer's decision about whether the comparison is enough for the renewal analysis.

A publication makes the distinction particularly clear. Gazette can compose an article from its source, resolve its links and produce the pages. The person preparing the site can inspect those pages and establish that the requested build was delivered correctly. The publisher still has to accept the finished words, images and claims for release. After release, checking what readers actually receive answers another question again. A successful build is evidence for that sequence, not a substitute for the publisher's judgement.

In Pale's design, coordination keeps those results with the work they establish and leaves each acceptance with its owner. The same distinction matters when people use Gazette directly: a draft can be ready for review while publication remains undecided.

Closing the handoff

Making both events explicit gives each party something practical. The analyst can see that the handoff landed without waiting for it to stand for the entire decision. The recipient can see the outcome still needing judgement without erasing the work already delivered. If more work is needed, its reason can be stated precisely. A workflow closes through that agreement, rather than through whichever party first writes "done".
