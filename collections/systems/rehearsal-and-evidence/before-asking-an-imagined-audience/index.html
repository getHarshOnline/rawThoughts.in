An imagined objection, a better question

A library wants more predictable staffing. Some visitors want the certainty of a booked slot; others cannot predict when they can come. Making appointments mandatory helps one part of the problem while possibly worsening another.

Try the proposal in an imagined conversation. One visitor needs to know a slot will be waiting after a long journey; another cannot know when work will let them leave. Now the library has a more useful question than “Will people like appointments?” Could it offer reliable bookings while keeping room for walk-ins?

A decision someone can revise

In this hypothetical library, the decision is whether appointments for its collection service should be optional or mandatory. Staff time is limited, and some visitors cannot plan ahead. Those alternatives give the conversation something to change. Without them, it can generate approval or criticism while leaving the library no closer to a choice. Asking for unlimited opening hours would dodge the staffing problem altogether.

A scenario should therefore state the proposal, the decision it supports, the affected contexts and the assumptions being tested. In RawFish’s planned workflow, these commitments feed the world graph, cohort choices and the report’s questions, so the imagined exchange can be examined through the conditions that produced it. A suggested interpretation of the operator's prose remains a suggestion until the operator accepts it. Otherwise the system may answer a more convenient question than the one the person intended.

The premises exposed by rehearsal

The two visitors' objections point in opposite directions. Neither tells us how many actual visitors have that need, but together they give the library something to investigate. Would an optional appointment lane preserve walk-in access? Does the current service already exclude some visitors? Speaking with visitors, examining service records and trying an alternative could distinguish an inconvenience from a substantial access barrier.

Give the proposal its strongest case, too. A rehearsal that only generates objections can be as biased as one that only generates praise. Limited staffing may make some form of appointment useful; keeping every option open may itself make the service less reliable. Both possibilities belong in the comparison:

Declared scenario condition Imagined response Question for the real investigation
Mandatory booking; some visitors have unpredictable schedules “I cannot know my free time in advance.” Which visitors depend on unplanned access, and what would they lose?
Optional booking; booked slots may be displaced by walk-in demand “I travel a long way and need the slot to be reliable.” Can staffing preserve a promised slot while retaining walk-in access?

These responses are written examples, not observations from generated runs or visitor interviews. Their role is to make two competing requirements inspectable. A good pre-mortem tests that trade-off rather than rewarding whichever side wrote the prompt.

The origin of a finding

A generated statement, an analyst's inference, an external source and an operator's assumption are different kinds of material. They can contribute to one report, but they should not be blended into a single evidential voice. “A simulated visitor objected to mandatory booking” describes generated behaviour. “Mandatory booking excludes visitors” is a claim about the world requiring its own evidence. The second does not follow merely because the first was vivid. The distinction must remain visible beside the finding, not just in a distant disclaimer. An extracted chart or quotation should not become easier to misrepresent than the full report.

Caution

Generated voices are not testimony. Real people must not be assigned invented reactions, and a generated public-policy discussion must not escape the tool as counterfeit citizen testimony.

The rehearsal returns us to the real library

The library still has to find out who misses visits and why. Conversations with visitors facing unpredictable schedules could distinguish a booking inconvenience from an access barrier. A comparison of optional and mandatory booking could reveal whether the promised staffing benefit survives without excluding walk-ins. Each observation bears on a different part of the decision; a synthetic approval percentage would settle none of them.

A rehearsal has helped when the next conversation with a visitor asks a better question: whether they need a predictable slot, an unplanned visit or a way to change the appointment. The imagined exchange has then returned something useful to the real library, without standing in for the people who use it.
